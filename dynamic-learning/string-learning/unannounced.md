# Saving useful weights without a boundary signal

**Result, November 25, 2026.** A learner with no curriculum-boundary signal selected a past projection that improved its ability to learn new programs from the earlier family. In 16 lifetimes the gain over keeping only current weights was **5.64 points** (paired 95% interval +4.35 to +6.95). The cost on the most recent family was **0.30 points** (−0.53 to −0.07). The prespecified capability and feedback comparisons passed. Scores are balanced accuracy, averaged over 4, 8 and 16 new demonstrations. The comparison concerns transformer representations in a finite string domain.

| Snapshot used with the current representation | Earlier family | Recent family |
|---|---:|---:|
| Current weights alone | 71.72% | 79.08% |
| Saved at a uniform random time | 75.59% | 78.93% |
| Saved at episode 120 | 75.84% | 78.88% |
| Saved at a different lifetime's selected time | 75.59% | 78.96% |
| Saved using this lifetime's past examples | **77.36%** | **78.78%** |
| Supplied the true boundary | 78.08% | 78.57% |
| Separate reference using explicit position and count features | 81.29% | 81.46% |

**Where the gain came from.** Every ten episodes we tested which of two previous projections still helped fit tasks sampled from a reservoir of verified experience. The selected time was a median 25 episodes from the hidden switch, versus 50 for the fixed midpoint. The learner could choose a useful representation without accurately estimating when the switch occurred. It saved anywhere from 130 episodes before to 100 after the switch. The test-time router initially favored current weights three to one. After 16 examples the archive's mean share was 61% on earlier-family tasks and 14% on recent-family tasks. Giving both projections equal initial odds bought 1.55 more points on the earlier family and cost 0.85 on the recent one. Pooling the two kernels scored 78.78% and 77.32%, respectively.

**The timing control mattered.** In the registered comparison, this lifetime's decision beat another lifetime's by 1.77 points (+0.72 to +2.99) on earlier-family tasks. The gain was 3.14 points for early switches and 0.41 for late ones. A post-hoc audit crossed every selected time with every lifetime. Against the mean of all other selections the gain was 1.47 points, or 1.37 points when restricted to lifetimes starting in the same family. Against the other replicate with the *same* order and switch time, the difference fell to 0.34 points (−0.52 to +1.30). The evidence principally supports adapting to the stream's schedule. A single globally preferred time chosen retrospectively using the other lifetimes' test outcomes came within 0.56 points of the learner (difference −0.65 to +1.51). The audit also reproduced the selected decision trace and primary task scores for every lifetime. [Crossed timing audit](timing-audit.json)

**How much experience was saved?** On earlier-family tasks the learner reached 77.23% after eight demonstrations. Current weights alone reached 77.51% after 16. This exploratory cross-budget difference was −0.28 points (−1.89 to +1.33). On the extra exclusive-or and count-equality operations, the average gains over current alone were +1.20 and +0.01 points. A successful strategy for recovering specialization left count equality near chance.

**When advice was wrong.** Both methods received the same checked sources and the same advice. On earlier-family queries both initially got right, the selected archive reduced harmful flips by 0.83 points under stable reliability and 1.00 after reversal. On jointly incorrect queries, truthful repairs increased 2.12 and 2.26 points. All recent-family differences were within 0.2 points in magnitude. These are conditional changes in readout behavior under simulated feedback. [Trial summaries and intervals](unannounced-summary.json)

**Costs and limitations.** The policy retains an archived projection, a temporary ten-episode challenger, and up to 24 previously seen episodes of 24 labeled examples each. It runs a small ridge fit and a 16-example check for each reservoir episode at candidate comparisons. We logged all candidate projections during training for counterfactual evaluation; only the archive and challenger influence the online decisions. The deployment classifier uses the current and archived projections. Training still receives the supervised task identity. The readout knows when demonstrations for a new task begin. Pretraining families, switches and their relative frequency are engineered. Different timing and routing policies were part of the comparisons; this result does not establish the value of all such policies.

The first run encountered a test pool without examples of one answer for a count-equality rule. Before any metrics were read, we made eligibility require nine distinct potential demonstrations plus at least one independent query per answer, and excluded evaluation-pool aliases. Two seeds retained 11 count comparisons and five equality programs instead of 12 and six. All other seeds retained the full set. Program identifiers and choices are in the settings file.

Routing examples to stored experts and scoring experience by predicted interference have established precedents. Our measured comparison concerns *when* to save a small portion of a changing network and how that choice affects the ability to learn a new rule. [Expert Gate](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2017/html/Aljundi_Expert_Gate_Lifelong_CVPR_2017_paper.html) · [Maximally interfered retrieval](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2019/hash/15825aee15eb335cc13f9b559f166ee8-Abstract.html)

## Protocol recorded before training

Earlier experiments saved a transformer projection at an announced practice boundary. This experiment withholds family labels, stage counts and switch times from the snapshot selector. Use 16 independent lifetimes, seeds 701–716. Cross practice order with switch times of 50, 90, 150 and 190 episodes in a 240-episode stream. Assign these combinations in a seeded random balanced order. Task heads are still supplied to the supervised training objective. The selector sees only the current encoder and already observed strings and verified labels. It never sees task heads, string-rule names, development outcomes or evaluation data.

Every ten episodes after a 20-episode warm-up, compare the archive to a challenger projection from ten episodes ago. Attach each to today's backbone. For a uniform reservoir of at most 24 previous practice episodes, fit a fast output head on eight past examples, evaluate on 16 further examples from that episode, and measure its positive accuracy advantage over the current projection. Keep the projection whose average advantage is greater. Prefer the challenger on ties. This one-slot archive rule is meant to preserve skills that the current representation struggles to acquire. It also uses one temporary challenger and the reservoir. Evaluate the saved state at episode 240 on completely new programs and strings. The model knows the start of each new evaluation task.

Controls use the same lifetime and test observations. Compare saving at the fixed midpoint, a uniformly random checkpoint, the actual hidden boundary, and the time selected in a different lifetime. Also compare a single current projection, pooled old/current features and the handcrafted position/count baseline. For deployment, set initial odds of using the archived weights to 1:3 and update those odds from prediction log loss on the new demonstrations. Compare against even initial odds on precisely the same state. Choose the ridge penalties and evidence temperature on development data; use the same selection procedure for each save policy. No evaluation labels inform snapshot selection or routing.

**Predictions.** The experience-selected archive will improve new tasks in the earlier family by at least three points over current alone (lower paired 95% lifetime-bootstrap bound above +3), and keep losses on the recent family under two points (lower bound above −2). To claim that the save decision itself adds value, it must beat saving at the time selected in another lifetime on the earlier family, with a lower bound above zero. Report deviations from the hidden boundary and stratify by early and late switches. Test wildcard conjunctions and pairwise count comparisons, and report exclusive-or and count equality separately. Enumerate all 1,024 inputs to check program novelty.

Evaluate the same learner under correct and incorrect advice on the reserved seventeenth demonstration and score propagation to other strings. Compare it to the current-only learner with identical fresh verification. Use the existing eight-check estimator for each of two sources, the same stable and reversed source distributions, and jointly eligible queries. Require the upper bound on excess harmful flips below +2 and lower bound on truthful repair above −2 points in both families and environments. Compare raw learning curves after 4, 8 and 16 verified samples. Bootstrap whole lifetimes. This is a one-thread CPU study.

## Timing audit specified after the primary run

The selected archive beat the preassigned time from another lifetime by 1.77 points on earlier-family tasks. That comparison uses just one assignment, and neighboring comparisons reuse one another's timing decisions. Reconstruct every training trajectory, verify its selected timestamp and the primary trial scores, and evaluate **every selected timestamp in every lifetime** on the identical two primary task families. Compare each lifetime against all other lifetimes' times, then against times from other lifetimes starting in the same family. Finally condition on the exact training order *and* boundary to ask whether the selector adds information beyond knowing those design variables. The last contrast averages over just one other seed per design cell. Report differences and hold out each lifetime when selecting a single globally preferred time from the other lifetimes. This last reference consults their test scores, so it is a privileged post-hoc comparator. The audit uses the same 16 lifetimes; it is a sensitivity analysis. Also report the exploratory 8-example adaptive versus 16-example current-only contrast.
