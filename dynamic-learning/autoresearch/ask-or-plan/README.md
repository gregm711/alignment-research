# When should a router ask for evidence or plan longer?

## Protocol before development or test outcomes

Phase 2, 25 November 2026. The invoice pilot found different repair rates with the same predictor and different probes. This second domain tests whether a controller can allocate **one discretionary action** between computing a better answer from known information and acquiring a new fact. We train new controllers here; the invoice candidates' code is specific to invoices. No external services supply training or test data.

Two transit lines lead to the same destination. Each line has three lanes and a known sequence of stages. At each stage a traveler can continue in the same lane or transfer to the next lane cyclically, with independently drawn positive integer travel times. Both start in lane zero. Greedily take the cheaper immediate edge (stay on ties) to estimate each line's time. Give the strong cheap predictor an ordinary least-squares correction trained against exact line times using only a constant, number of stages, greedy path time and sum of the absolute differences of the two edge times along that path. Clip to the physical interval from one minute per stage to the feasible greedy time. The raw greedy predictor is an ablation. Neither cheap method sees the optimal time at test.

A randomly designated line has a reported probability of an additional known-size delay at departure. Fork every map into both possible true states, weighting them by that probability. The learner sees only the probability until it buys a live check. Choices are **stop** (cheap times and expected delay), **plan** (exact shortest times by backward dynamic programming and expected delay), and **ask** (cheap times and the actual delay). The route selector minimizes estimated travel time, taking line zero on ties. Once it selects a line, score its actual optimal transit time on that line plus any true delay; the traveler can follow the ordinary service's optimal itinerary. Thus the discretionary planning action improves the line choice, not execution after choosing. Also report **both** as an outside-budget reference. Charge all shared predictor work equally within each comparison. Report graph-edge evaluations, queries, and decision overhead separately. Action prices below are illustrative utility costs in equivalent minutes, not measurements of wall time or energy.

Use 1,536 training maps, 384 development maps and 1,024 fresh test maps for each of two regimes. Independent seed ranges start at 110000, 210000, 310000 and 410000. Training, development and standard test use 4–9 stages per line independently, edge times uniform integers 1–9, delay sizes 3, 7, 12 or 20 minutes, and calibrated delay probabilities 0.15, 0.35, 0.5 or 0.8. Shifted test uses 8–14 stages, outlier edges (15% chance of adding 10 minutes), delay sizes 2, 5, 9 or 16, and probabilities 0.1, 0.3, 0.65 or 0.9. Preserve all forks of a map together. Validate dynamic programming by exhaustive path enumeration on small graphs and compare the value of a probe to the closed-form expectation on the two branches. Save per-map paired action losses, decisions, estimated times, seed and true optimal times.

Primary cost is mean excess travel time over a traveler with exact planning and complete information, plus 0.5 minute for either discretionary action. Lower is better. At development time choose the best fixed action, the best cheap rule (ask when expected savings using cheap times exceeds the probe price, otherwise always stop or always plan), and a fitted decision tree (depth 1, 2 or 3). The tree minimizes cost on training maps, splits only at training feature quartiles, and requires 32 training maps per child. Available features are lengths, cheap travel-time gap relative to alerted line, probability, delay size, absolute expected gap, the estimated value of knowing the delay, and differences in the greedy corrections. Also give Codex at most two bounded proposals for a depth-three decision tree over the same features, with scalar thresholds and named action leaves. It sees the preregistration, feature descriptions, development summaries, and examples from development only. Archive hypotheses and trees before scoring each proposal. Select the lowest development cost across fitted and Codex trees; resolve ties in favor of the fitted method. Freeze selections before opening either test regime.

Prediction: the selected tree saves at least 0.15 equivalent minute per map over the development-selected fixed action on the standard test, with a paired 95% interval above zero. To claim a practical advantage it must also beat the development-selected cheap rule with an interval above zero. Report both comparisons on shifted maps without retuning. Shuffle its decisions across maps (seed 51) as a same-action-frequency control; compare to an oracle choosing the best permitted action for each map averaged over possible delays. Report all fixed actions, the two-action reference, cost-free excess travel, optimal-line rate (either line in a tie), probes and edge evaluations. Reprice the frozen decisions with check prices 0.25 and 2 minutes, holding planning price at 0.5. The router learned for the primary price may cease to pay at a different price. Bootstrap maps with 2,000 paired draws (seed 52). The design tests amortized action selection in a known finite domain. Out-of-domain transfer from invoices, general intelligence and electrical efficiency require other measurements.

CPU only, one numerical-library thread, 120 seconds per simulator invocation. Two read-only Codex proposal calls use the existing local CLI authentication and its 240-second timeout. No paid GPU runs.
