# Does experience change how one person's feedback should generalize?

## Protocol fixed before accessing ratings

Use real, previously unseen people's movie ratings as the test tasks. Historical users supply the training experience. No movie pairs or shared preferences are assigned by the experiment. Predict whether a person rated a movie four or five stars. This tests a recommendation setting where cross-item generalization is an established idea. It also lets us fork an actual correction, change its sign, and measure consequences on other ratings from the same person. This is a cold-start analogue of the routing study; these data do not identify a change in a person's true preferences.

Sort the 943 users by the time of their first rating, with user ID as the tie-break. Use the first 60% of users for history, the next 17% to select settings, and all remaining users for testing. Train only on historical ratings timestamped strictly before the first development user's first rating. Use the 160 most rated historical films and report eligible counts. Evaluate users with at least 20 distinct ratings on those films. Reveal their first 1, 2, 4 and 8 such ratings in timestamp order with a deterministic, label-independent tie-break. Score all remaining ratings after the eighth for identical query sets at every budget. Missing ratings have no labels.

Every learner begins with the same historical estimate of approval for each film. Compare (1) no adaptation, (2) a single learned user-specific leniency offset, (3) leniency plus genre similarity, (4) leniency plus a positive semidefinite similarity matrix estimated from covariation of observed historical rating residuals, (5) the same matrix with film identities scrambled, and (6) a standard low-rank collaborative factor model trained only on historical users. Fit fresh user weights by regularized least squares against residuals of the movie means. Use disjoint development users to tune regularization and the strength of each shared update channel by Brier score, including zero strength. Report test Brier score and accuracy, equally weighting users and support budgets. Give paired 95% user-bootstrap intervals. A strong practical gain requires a three-percentage-point accuracy advantage against the development-selected best of leniency and genre models with a lower bound above zero. Report differences from the collaborative factor model separately regardless of result.

After four ratings fork the fifth into the actual binary rating and its opposite. Compare effects on distinct later films, restricted to queries where both the historical covariance and conventional comparator were initially wrong (truthful repairs), or both were initially correct (harmful flips). Bootstrap jointly eligible counts by person. The joint threshold uses a lower bound above −2 points on relative repairs and an upper bound below +2 on excess harm. The feedback flip is a controlled perturbation, not an actual deceptive rating. Preserve only aggregate and anonymized per-person scores in the repository. Use the ratings, item IDs and genre tags locally, without user demographics. [GroupLens release](https://grouplens.org/datasets/movielens/100k/) · [Harper and Konstan, The MovieLens Datasets](https://doi.org/10.1145/2827872)

Censor development ratings at the first test user's start time as well, so model selection uses evidence that was available before any test task. The movie kernels are shared across users, and each user's final predictor can be exported as one 160-value head. Use 12 factors and 12 regularized alternating-least-squares sweeps for the collaborative reference. Count tied timestamps at the support/query boundary because they limit interpretation of temporal order.

## Passive result

Of 217 held-out users, 113 rated at least 20 movies in the historical catalog. The same rule left 103 development users. Forty-six test users had ratings with equal timestamps either side of the eighth-rating boundary. Timing records when someone submitted a rating, and multiple ratings could be entered together. Training used 24,923 observed ratings on the selected 160 films.

The covariance learner achieved 67.50% accuracy, up 0.93 percentage points over keeping all weights fixed (+0.24 to +1.64), but **down 0.23 points** versus genre and leniency (−0.53 to +0.07). Scrambling which film owned each covariance row and column changed accuracy by only 0.02 points (−0.20 to +0.25). Brier scores were 0.20977 for historical covariance and 0.20954 for genre. The low-rank collaborative reference scored 67.52%. The two-point feedback bounds passed, with a +0.40-point difference in truthful repairs (−0.41 to +1.23) and exactly zero net difference in harmful flips (−0.49 to +0.50). Neither conditional feedback result implies an overall accuracy gain. [Full results](ratings.json) · [Per-person score arrays](rating-trials.npz)

## Active follow-up fixed after the passive result

The policy-change study found two distinct effects, choosing a better check and using different weights to learn from the same check. Test both here. Start all arms from the exact same personalized 160-film head after the first four genuine ratings, using the genre learner. Offer the next eight rated films as possible questions. Keep their labels hidden. Score only films after those twelve. This is a retrospective menu conditioned on which films the user actually rated. Its responses are real, and a prospective system could encounter refusal or missing data.

Use the settings fitted in the primary study. Either the genre or covariance model picks the film whose label would most reduce its estimated predictive variance averaged uniformly across the *historical catalog*. Every reader gets the same starting score for every candidate. Cross selector and reader, with the reader using its own conditional kernel for a single recursive ridge step. Compare to exact uniform choice over the eight possible checks and to updating only general leniency. Fork the selected rating into its real value and its opposite. Score all future films, counting harmful flips and repairs only among jointly eligible examples. Require the algebraic one-step genre update to reproduce a full five-rating refit for every candidate before interpreting any results. Report user-paired intervals on accuracy and Brier score and count how frequently choices disagree. The three-point gain and two-point feedback bounds still apply. All settings, data and users are reused. This tests a specified mechanism on the same cohort.

## Active result

The selectors disagreed for 101 of 113 people. The covariance selector and reader together changed accuracy by **−0.25 percentage points** relative to the genre selector and reader (−0.94 to +0.44); Brier score changed by −0.00026 (−0.00243 to +0.00187). Starting from the same weights and fixing the covariance selector's choice, switching only the reader changed accuracy by −0.33 points (−0.73 to +0.03). Under those matched inputs the covariance reader made 0.45 points more harmful flips (+0.03 to +0.83) and 0.49 points more truthful repairs (−0.18 to +1.15). Uniformly averaging over all eight possible checks with the covariance reader gave similar overall accuracy to selecting one. The variance-based selector is model-based and here estimates the value of information across the whole catalog, whereas users rated self-selected films.

All 904 one-step genre updates reproduced a full five-rating refit to absolute tolerance 1e−12. The model adapts a small numerical preference head. The fixed public ratings and these illustrative single-bit feedback interventions support a limited statement about cold-start learning. They provide no measurement of an individual's preferences changing over time or of an assistant resisting persuasion. ## Reproduce

From this directory run `python3 ratings.py` then `python3 active.py` with NumPy installed. The first command downloads the public archive into the local research cache and checks its SHA-256. The scripts read only the ratings and movie metadata. The custom user split and binary scoring differ from the dataset's standard evaluation. Data from GroupLens Research, University of Minnesota.
