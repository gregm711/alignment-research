# What should one correction change elsewhere?

## Protocol recorded before running

A fictional service has two queues and six familiar request types. Its queue policy changes over time. Some types used to change together, for reasons independent of the wording. Compare two services with identical requests and identical current weights, but different histories of which changes occur together. The causal question is whether past experience makes a correction to one type change the deployed weight for the right *other* type. CLINC150 supplies authentic requests. Queue policies, change histories, and sources are constructed.

For each of 20 fixed lifetimes (seeds 901–920, two per CLINC domain), sample six intents. Estimate six semantic prototypes from 32 official training requests each. Keep them fixed throughout the lifetime. Class probabilities use cosine similarity and a temperature chosen by log loss on distinct official validation requests from these six intents. Only the historical audit sees which intent assignments actually changed. Each of 48 historical audits verifies a complete six-bit mask. Forty percent of audits change nothing. Others flip just one of three arbitrary intent pairs. Balance intent exposure by sampling pairs at random. A second history permutes the records onto disjoint pairs. It has exactly the same event sizes. At the start of every test both arms deploy the same old policy.

Learn 15 nonnegative pairwise weights and six offsets by fitting the likelihood of the historical audit masks over the 64 possible changes. Compare independent per-intent change probabilities, the same model given the other service's history, a smoothed empirical distribution of entire historical changes, and a model given the true pairing. Also test a mixture that assigns half its prior mass to independent changes. These priors are fixed before the new incident. As evidence arrives, use its likelihood to write six new queue probabilities. Future inference needs only those six weights and the fixed intent recognizer. Test wording of support and query requests must be disjoint. Test requests are never used to fit any historical parameters.

At evaluation, equally weight old policies that remain valid, single-pair changes, and simultaneous two-pair changes never seen during practice. Measure paired accuracy after 1, 2, 4 and 8 independently sourced corrections (10% independent label error), both when the historical pairing persists and when a disjoint pairing governs the new incident. Use identical observations and query strings for all methods. Score at least 96 incidents per lifetime in each regime. Report the causal timing curve and each change type separately. The capability target is a mean gain above three percentage points over independent updates on novel two-pair changes under the historical pairing, with the lower paired 95% lifetime bootstrap bound above zero. Report the empirical baseline and lost performance under changed coupling.

For the feedback test, make a true paired change and a no-change incident look identical. Show the same contrary report about one intent in both futures. Probe the historical partner using other human requests, restricting to those correctly routed under the old policy. Supply a second contrary report from the same persistent source, then separately from an independent source. Repeating the same source on the same request supplies no new evidence. A genuinely independent reporter has a 10% error rate. Score truthful repairs, harmful flips, and their difference versus independent updating. The joint target requires the upper interval for additional harmful flips to stay below two points and the lower interval for loss of truthful repairs above minus two. Finally reveal a separate verified example of the partner, correct in each fork, and remeasure. Report any equivalence forced by the identical evidence. These probes test a statistical learner's response to feedback under a known source model.

Run locally with one BLAS thread. Preserve seed-level outcomes, sample assignments, trained coupling weights, and the final emitted weights for a reproducible incident. Intervals describe variation in these constructed lifetimes on a single corpus.

## What happened

On the new combination of two historical pairs, the coupled learner scored **69.01%** versus **56.72%** for independent changes, a gain of 12.28 percentage points (95% paired lifetime interval +11.61 to +12.86; rounded from the unrounded difference). Merely remembering the empirical distribution of past incidents gained 1.42 points. Giving the coupled learner the other service's history cost 15.73 points compared with giving it its own. No practice audit contained a two-pair change. With one old pair changing the coupled gain was 5.79 points. With no changes it lost 0.61 points. After an unannounced change in pairings, the old coupling lost 9.62 points on single changes and 3.79 on double changes. Thus learning the structure bought transfer and created a specific vulnerability.

On the historical partner, a single contrary report produced 87.11% truthful repairs and exactly the same fraction of harmful flips in the matched false-report fork. Independent updating produced 0.37% of each. Conditional on receiving the identical report, both futures have identical inputs, including the previous weights. No deterministic or randomized learner can separate them in expectation until it gets distinguishing evidence. These conditional rates are not a deployment error rate. After a second independent reporter and a verified example of the partner, coupled updating still produced 4.69 points more harmful flips than independent updating (+2.67 to +6.84), missing the two-point bound. ## Follow-up specified after seeing the matched-fork failure

Give the learner a choice of which fresh request to check. The verifier returns the correct queue. At each step offer one unseen official test request from each of six intents, in random order. The learner sees their semantic features only. The evaluation uses different official test requests. Begin with one unsolicited report. Compare uniform checks, the most uncertain candidate, and the check predicted to minimize expected classification errors across the six intent assignments. The last policy averages over both possible verified outcomes using the current posterior. Let independent, coupled and half-independent priors each choose their own checks. Include a baseline that verifies the request most similar in intent probabilities to the unsolicited report. All approaches receive the same number of checks on identical streams of candidates.

Use 20 additional histories (seeds 951–970) with new sampled intent sets, prototype examples, wording and historical audits. Reuse the six intent setup, rates, and change regimes above. Score after 1, 2 and 4 requested verifications; make 48 new incidents per change count, regime and lifetime. Record the first candidate chosen and the posterior probability of the familiar coupling component before and after checks in the half-independent mixture. Test whether that probability distinguishes familiar from changed pairings. Also rerun both feedback forks with identical first reports and candidate menus, now allowing the verified answer to differ across the two futures. Compare all choices against independently updating weights with the same choice policy. The primary learner uses the half-independent prior and expected-error selection. To pass jointly, it must gain three points on novel combinations with the familiar pairing (lower interval above zero), have a lower interval above −2 on those combos under changed pairing, and after one check have upper excess harm below +2 and lower excess truthful repair above −2. Report empirical results even if some targets fail. This procedure assumes access to an accurate verifier and a selectable pool.

## Results with selectable verification

The half-independent learner using expected-error selection met all four specified criteria. Across 20 fresh histories it gained **13.93 points** over independently updating weights that also chose their checks on new combinations of familiar pairs (+13.18 to +14.66), averaging 1, 2 and 4 checks. After the pairings changed, it lost **0.93 points** (−1.52 to −0.31). The average conceals a 2.6-point deficit at four checks in that changed regime. It also lost **3.93 points** (−4.42 to −3.43) on changed single-pair incidents, outside the joint primary criterion. Against uniform checks with the same learner, choosing checks gained 6.43 points on familiar double changes and 3.24 on changed doubles. After four checks on double changes the average posterior mass on the historical component was 89.6% with the original pairing and 28.0% when it changed. This is evidence about the constructed pairing model, conditional on the history and verifier assumptions.

With one check after the same contrary report, truthful partner repairs rose from 16.5% to 86.3%, and harmful partner flips fell from 1.0% to 0.2%. A verifier could now supply genuinely different evidence across futures. The adaptive policy chose the historical partner 71.5% of the time versus 15.9% with the independent model. The independent baseline therefore often saw *different evidence*. The preceding comparison measures the end-to-end benefit of selecting and interpreting a check. ## Causal follow-up specified after the verification result

On 20 fresh seeds (1001–1020), cross who picks the first check with who updates the weights. Either independent or historically coupled (half-independent mixture) machinery selects the check by expected error. Give *both readers* exactly that text and answer, starting from the same initial report and old policy. Report all four cells, including the change from switching only the reader and switching only the selector, on matched truthful and misleading reports and on double changes with familiar and changed pairs. As a second intervention, corrupt the returned verification with probability 10%, independently of the first report, with identical flips for every reader of a given check. Compare readers told the correct error rate to readers still assuming perfect verification. Use 96 primary incidents and 120 paired feedback probes per seed. The first choice is made under the original perfect-verifier assumption in all conditions to isolate feedback reliability from selection. If the lower interval on the truthful-repair difference from switching readers alone is below +3 points, attribute no robust three-point benefit to that change. Report how often the *first two* independent checks disagree with the historical grouping only if directly measured; keep explanations grounded in the factorial comparisons.

## Results when both readers see the same check

With the adaptive selector fixed and an accurate check, changing only the reader raised truthful repairs from 66.71% to 83.36%, a 16.65-point gain (+11.33 to +21.96). This isolates an effect of using history to write the six routing weights even after controlling for what was learned from the verifier. On novel combinations of familiar pairs the same change raised accuracy 7.15 points (+6.54 to +7.73). Holding the independent reader fixed and changing only the selector raised truthful repairs by 52.58 points (+40.98 to +63.23). The effects interact.

Verifier errors reversed the harm conclusion. Under 10% independent corruption, informing both readers of the correct error rate left the adaptive system with **7.41% harmful partner flips** versus **1.06%** in the independent system, a difference of +6.35 points (+4.66 to +8.10). With accurate checks the difference was −0.03 points (−0.15 to +0.08). Accurate calibration alone did not recover the two-point bound. All rates are conditional on the matched false-report probes; routine deployment rates depend on how often those reports occur.
